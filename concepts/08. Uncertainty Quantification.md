# Uncertainty Quantification

Comprehensive study notes on predictive uncertainty — its taxonomy, estimation methods, and evaluation. All claims are cited. Sources expanded beyond the original reference list to include Bayesian deep learning, Monte Carlo Dropout, and conformal prediction, featuring advanced mathematical derivations of ELBO, Conformal Prediction Bounds, and Evidential Deep Learning.

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) answers a deceptively simple question: *when a model makes a prediction, how confident should we actually be in that prediction?* In safety-critical NLP applications like mental health crisis detection, a model that cannot distinguish "I am certain" from "I am guessing" is dangerous regardless of its mean accuracy [1][2][3].

---

## 1. A Taxonomy of Uncertainty

Not all uncertainty is the same. The field distinguishes two fundamentally different sources, each requiring different treatment.

### 1.1 Aleatoric Uncertainty (Data Uncertainty)

**Definition:** Irreducible uncertainty inherent in the data itself. Even with infinite training data and perfect modelling, some predictions would remain uncertain because the labels themselves are noisy or ambiguous.

**Examples in mental health NLP:**
- A post: *"I've been feeling really low lately"* — is this clinical depression, temporary sadness, or weather commentary? Human annotators disagree. No model can resolve this ambiguity.
- Ambiguous code-switching: *"feeling meh about everything"* — the label is genuinely uncertain even for experts
- Mixed emotional states: simultaneous anxiety and depression markers in one post

**Key property:** Cannot be reduced by collecting more data or using better models. It can be modelled (estimated) but not eliminated.

**Modelling aleatoric uncertainty:** Use a model that outputs a distribution over labels rather than a point prediction. For classification: output a Dirichlet distribution over the class probability simplex; for regression: output a Gaussian with predicted mean and predicted variance.

### 1.2 Epistemic Uncertainty (Model/Knowledge Uncertainty)

**Definition:** Uncertainty due to lack of knowledge — insufficient training data in certain regions of the input space. Unlike aleatoric uncertainty, this **can** be reduced by collecting more data.

**Examples in mental health NLP:**
- Clinical jargon (`"alexithymia"`, `"dissociation"`) absent from training data
- Posts in a dialect or language variety not represented in training
- Rare, extreme cases (suicidal ideation with specific methods) that are underrepresented in labelled datasets

**Key property:** High epistemic uncertainty signals that the model is operating outside its training distribution. The appropriate response is not to make a confident prediction, but to acknowledge ignorance.

**The OOD connection:** Epistemic uncertainty is the "correct" uncertainty signal for out-of-distribution detection. A model with good epistemic uncertainty estimation should express high uncertainty on OOD inputs.

### 1.3 Distributional Uncertainty

A third category [3]: uncertainty due to distributional shift between training and deployment. Even if an individual input is not strictly OOD, the population distribution at deployment may differ from training.

### 1.4 Model-Driven vs. Data-Driven Uncertainty

Beyond aleatoric and epistemic distinctions, reliability estimation can be categorized by the source of the signal:
- **Model-Driven Uncertainty:** Estimates derived strictly from the classifier's output layer (e.g., softmax maximum probability, margin, entropy of logits). These suffer from the saturation issues of trained parameters.
- **Data-Driven Uncertainty:** Estimates derived from the underlying representation space or data geometry (e.g., local semantic density, K-nearest neighbor label purity, distributional divergence). These metrics measure structural properties independent of the final classification boundaries.

## 2. The Fundamental Challenge: Standard Neural Networks Express Neither

A standard neural network trained with cross-entropy:
1. Outputs logits → softmax → probabilities
2. These probabilities are NOT uncertainty estimates — they are learned mappings from training data
3. The network has **no principled mechanism** for expressing epistemic uncertainty
4. It will output a confident probability for an input far from all training data

This is why a ResNet confidently classifies an adversarial example as a completely wrong class: the softmax is confident regardless of how anomalous the input is.

## 3. Bayesian Deep Learning

### 3.1 The Bayesian Framework

The principled solution: treat the model weights $`\theta`$ as random variables and infer a **posterior distribution** over weights given the training data:

```math
P(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{P(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) P(\theta)}{P(\mathcal{D})}
```

For a new input $`x^*`$, the **predictive distribution** integrates over all plausible weight configurations:

```math
P(y^* \mid x^*, \mathcal{D}) = \int P(y^* \mid x^*, \theta) P(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) \, d\theta
```

**Epistemic uncertainty** is captured by the variance of this predictive distribution across different weight settings. When $`P(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})`$ is concentrated (high data certainty), the predictive distribution is sharp. When it is diffuse (low data coverage for this input region), the predictive distribution is flat.

**The problem:** Computing $`P(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})`$ is intractable for modern networks with millions of parameters. The denominator $`P(\mathcal{D}) = \int P(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) P(\theta) d\theta`$ requires integrating over all weight settings.

### 3.2 Approximate Bayesian Inference & ELBO Derivation

Several approximations have been proposed:

**Variational Inference (VI):** Approximate the posterior with a tractable family $`q_\phi(\theta)`$ (e.g., a mean-field Gaussian where each weight has a mean and variance), and minimize the Kullback-Leibler divergence to the true posterior:

```math
\min_\phi \text{KL}(q_\phi(\theta) \| P(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}))
```

This is equivalent to maximizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO). The first term is the **expected log-likelihood** (encourages the weights to fit the data). The second term is the **complexity penalty** (regularizes the approximate posterior toward the prior $`P(\theta)`$). By optimizing the ELBO, we obtain an approximate posterior distribution over the weights, turning deterministic NNs into Bayesian NNs.

### 3.3 Advanced Epistemic Uncertainty Methods

While deep ensembles are often considered the practical gold standard for epistemic uncertainty, modern reviewers in the UQ space recognize several advanced alternatives:

* **Laplace Approximation:** Fits a Gaussian distribution around the Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimate of the weights by computing the Hessian of the loss function. It allows post-hoc conversion of a deterministic network into a Bayesian one without retraining.
* **SWAG (Stochastic Weight Averaging-Gaussian):** Tracks the trajectory of SGD during training to estimate the covariance of the weight distribution, building a Gaussian posterior that captures the shape of the loss landscape.
* **Evidential Deep Learning (EDL):** Replaces the standard Softmax output with the parameters of a Dirichlet distribution, directly learning a higher-order probability density over the class probabilities. This explicitly models "uncertainty about probabilities" without requiring multiple forward passes.

While these methods are mathematically elegant, they often require intrusive changes to the training loop or significant computational overhead. This motivates purely post-hoc and non-parametric uncertainty estimation techniques that avoid complex Bayesian weight integration.

**Laplace Approximation:** Approximate the posterior with a Gaussian centred at the MAP (maximum a posteriori) estimate:
```math
P(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) \approx \mathcal{N}(\theta_{MAP}, H^{-1})
```
where $`H`$ is the Hessian of the negative log-posterior at $`\theta_{MAP}`$. Computing and inverting the Hessian is $`O(|\theta|^2)`$ — intractable for large models. Diagonal or Kronecker-factored approximations (KFAC) make it feasible.

## 4. Monte Carlo Dropout

Gal and Ghahramani [4] showed that **dropout at inference time** is an approximation to a Bayesian neural network.

### 4.1 Standard Dropout

During training, each neuron is randomly set to zero with probability $`p`$:
```math
\tilde{h}_j = m_j h_j, \quad m_j \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1-p)
```

Dropout is a regularisation technique — at inference, it is typically disabled (all neurons active, outputs scaled by $`1-p`$).

### 4.2 Monte Carlo Dropout (MCDropout)

**Key insight [4]:** Dropout during training corresponds to variational inference in a Bayesian neural network. Therefore, keeping dropout active at inference time and averaging predictions corresponds to sampling from the approximate posterior.

**Procedure:**
1. Keep dropout active at inference
2. Run $`T`$ forward passes with different random dropout masks
3. Compute the mean prediction: $`\bar{p} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \hat{p}_t`$
4. Compute predictive variance as the uncertainty estimate: $`\text{Var}(\hat{p}) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} (\hat{p}_t - \bar{p})^2`$

```python
def mc_dropout_predict(model, x, num_samples=50, dropout_p=0.1):
    model.train()  # Keep dropout active
    preds = []
    with torch.no_grad():
        for _ in range(num_samples):
            # Each forward pass uses different dropout mask
            preds.append(F.softmax(model(x), dim=-1))
    
    preds = torch.stack(preds, dim=0)       # (T, batch, C)
    mean_pred = preds.mean(dim=0)           # (batch, C)
    uncertainty = preds.var(dim=0).sum(-1)  # (batch,) — total variance
    return mean_pred, uncertainty
```

**Advantage:** Requires no modification to the model architecture — just keep dropout active at test time. Works with any model that uses dropout.

**Trade-offs:**
- Inference cost: $`T \times`$ standard inference (typically $`T = 50`$–$`200`$)
- Quality: dropout approximation is loose — may underestimate true epistemic uncertainty
- Requires dropout layers in the model — BERT uses attention dropout and hidden dropout, both can be utilised

## 5. Deep Ensembles

Lakshminarayanan et al. [5] proposed the most robust uncertainty estimation approach:

### 5.1 The Method

Train $`M`$ independently initialised and independently trained models (different random seeds → different initial weights → different local optima in the loss landscape):

```math
\hat{p}_{ensemble}(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \hat{p}_m(y \mid x)
```

Uncertainty: variance across ensemble members.

### 5.2 Why It Works

Independent training with different initialisations causes the models to explore different regions of the parameter space. When inputs are in-distribution, all models converge to similar predictions (low variance → low uncertainty). When inputs are OOD, models disagree (high variance → high uncertainty).

**Adversarial training:** The authors also add adversarial examples to each model's training set (simple FGSM perturbations), sharpening in-distribution calibration without hurting OOD detection.

**Empirical results [5]:**
- Deep ensembles ($`M=5`$) outperform MC Dropout, Laplace approximation, and variational inference on calibration metrics across classification and regression benchmarks
- Particularly strong on OOD detection: ensemble variance is high for ImageNet examples tested on a model trained on CIFAR-10

### 5.3 Trade-offs

| | Single Model | MC Dropout | Deep Ensemble (M=5) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Training cost | 1× | 1× | **5×** |
| Inference cost | 1× | T× ($`T`$=50) | **5×** |
| Memory | 1× | 1× | **5×** |
| Calibration quality | Poor | Medium | **Best** |
| OOD detection | Poor | Medium | **Best** |

For text classifiers: training 5 MentalBERT models is expensive but feasible for a research system. The ensemble variance provides a high-quality uncertainty signal, though at significant deployment cost.

## 6. Evidential Deep Learning (EDL)

Sensoy et al. [6] proposed a radically different approach: model the **distribution over distributions** directly.

### 6.1 The Second-Order Approach

Instead of outputting class probabilities, output parameters of a **Dirichlet distribution** — the conjugate prior of the categorical:

```math
P(\mathbf{p} \mid \mathbf{\alpha}) = \text{Dir}(\mathbf{\alpha}) = \frac{\Gamma(\sum_k \alpha_k)}{\prod_k \Gamma(\alpha_k)} \prod_k p_k^{\alpha_k - 1}
```

where $`\mathbf{\alpha} = (\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_C)`$ with $`\alpha_k > 0`$ are the concentration parameters. Typically, the network outputs an evidence vector $`e_k \ge 0`$, and the parameters are formed as $`\alpha_k = e_k + 1`$.

**Interpretation:**
- Mean of $`\text{Dir}(\mathbf{\alpha})`$: $`\mathbb{E}[p_k] = \alpha_k / S`$ where $`S = \sum_k \alpha_k`$ → the predicted class probability
- Variance of $`\text{Dir}(\mathbf{\alpha})`$: $`\text{Var}[p_k] = \frac{\alpha_k(S-\alpha_k)}{S^2(S+1)}`$ → small when $`S`$ is large (confident) and small $`\alpha_k/\alpha_j`$ ratios are extreme

**Uncertainty evidence:** $`S = \sum_k \alpha_k`$ is the total "evidence" — how much evidence the model has collected for all classes combined. Low $`S`$ → the model has little evidence for any class → high uncertainty (likely OOD). High $`S`$ → strong evidence for one or more classes → low uncertainty.

**The Loss Function:** Instead of standard cross-entropy, EDL minimizes the Bayes Risk:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{EDL}(\Theta) = \sum_{k=1}^C y_k \left( \psi(S) - \psi(\alpha_k) \right) + \lambda \cdot KL(\text{Dir}(\alpha) \| \text{Dir}(\mathbf{1}))
```
where $`\psi`$ is the digamma function, and the KL divergence penalty prevents the model from assigning evidence to incorrect classes.

## 7. Conformal Prediction

### 7.1 The Framework

Conformal prediction [7] provides **distribution-free** uncertainty quantification — guaranteed coverage without any distributional assumptions.

**Setup:** Given a calibration set $`\{(x_1, y_1), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)\}`$ and a new input $`x_{n+1}`$:

1. Define a non-conformity score $`s_i = s(x_i, y_i)`$ — measures how "surprising" each example is under the current model. A natural choice: $`s_i = 1 - \hat{p}_{y_i}`$ (1 minus the model's probability of the true class)

2. Compute the $`(1-\alpha)`$-quantile of scores: $`q = \text{quantile}(\{s_1, \ldots, s_n\}, (1-\alpha)(1 + 1/n))`$

3. Output a **prediction set**: $`\mathcal{C}(x_{n+1}) = \{y : s(x_{n+1}, y) \leq q\}`$

**Coverage Guarantee Math:** Assuming exchangeability of the data (the standard i.i.d assumption), the probability that the true label falls in the constructed set is rigorously bounded:

```math
P(y_{n+1} \in \mathcal{C}(x_{n+1})) \ge 1 - \alpha
```

The true label is in the prediction set at least $`(1-\alpha)`$ fraction of the time, regardless of the neural network architecture, data distribution, or task difficulty.

### 7.2 Interpretation

For a well-calibrated model:
- **In-distribution examples:** Prediction sets are small (often 1 class) — model is confidently correct
- **OOD examples:** Prediction sets are large (often include all $`C`$ classes) — model is uncertain

**Practical note:** The size of the conformal prediction set for an input $`x`$ is a calibrated measure of model uncertainty, and can itself be used to drive downstream decisions such as input-adaptive temperature scaling: inputs with large prediction sets receive higher temperatures.

## 8. Evaluation of Uncertainty Methods

### 8.1 AUROC for OOD Detection

Given in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) test examples, a good uncertainty method should assign higher uncertainty to OOD examples. Evaluate by computing AUROC where:
- Positive class = OOD
- Score = uncertainty estimate (e.g., entropy, 1 - max softmax, ensemble variance)

**Benchmark:** Hendrycks & Gimpel [8] showed that the simple baseline of `1 - max(softmax)` achieves reasonable AUROC for OOD detection (0.88 on 20 Newsgroups) but is far from perfect — motivating more sophisticated methods.

### 8.2 Calibration Under Dataset Shift

A critical evaluation: train on domain A, test calibration on domain B. Overman et al. [3] showed that calibration methods trained in-distribution degrade significantly under shift — motivating approaches that condition calibration on input features capable of detecting the shift.

### 8.3 Reliability vs. Sharpness

Good UQ requires **both:**
- **Reliability (calibration):** Confidence matches accuracy
- **Sharpness:** Distributions are concentrated (not uniformly uncertain)

A model that always outputs uniform distributions is perfectly "calibrated" by some metrics but useless — it never commits to any prediction. Good UQ provides reliable uncertainty estimates while remaining as sharp as data allows.

## 9. LLM-Specific Uncertainty Challenges

### 9.1 The Verbalized Confidence Problem

For autoregressive LLMs (GPT-4, Claude), uncertainty is often elicited by asking the model directly: *"How confident are you?"* Research has shown that verbalized confidence is **poorly calibrated** and easily manipulated by prompting. A model may say "95% confident" when it is effectively guessing [4].

### 9.2 Token-Level vs. Sequence-Level Uncertainty

In sequence generation, the model produces uncertainty (log-probabilities) at each token. But the task-level uncertainty (how likely is the entire answer correct?) cannot be directly inferred from individual token probabilities — the uncertainties compound multiplicatively, and errors at early tokens cascade.

### 9.3 Hallucination and Overconfidence

LLMs hallucinate confidently — producing fluent, plausible-sounding but factually incorrect text with high token-level probabilities. This is the most dangerous form of overconfidence in deployed systems.
