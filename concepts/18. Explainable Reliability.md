# Explainable Reliability

These are comprehensive study notes. Every factual claim is cited. References focus on the intersection of Explainable AI (XAI) and uncertainty quantification, with a specific emphasis on clinical and high-stakes decision-making.

If a reliability estimator predicts that a model is going to fail, the immediate next question a clinician will ask is: *"Why?"* 

This note covers the methodologies for explaining reliability signals to end-users, contrasting standard post-hoc feature attribution with the inherent explainability of example-based ($k$-NN) methods.

### Conceptual Hierarchy

```text
The Need for Explanation
          ↓
  Intrinsic vs. Post-Hoc XAI
          ↓
  Feature Attribution (SHAP/LIME)
          ↓
  Example-Based Explanations (k-NN Retrieval)
          ↓
  Clinical Trust & Usability
```

---

## 1. The Clinical Need for Explainability

In high-stakes domains like mental health diagnosis or medical triage, an AI abstention ("I don't know") is not enough. If the system flags a patient's note as "Unreliable Prediction," it must provide actionable context so the human-in-the-loop can resolve the ambiguity.

Rudin [1] famously argued that high-stakes decisions should completely abandon black-box models with post-hoc explainers in favor of models that are *intrinsically* interpretable.

## 2. Intrinsic vs. Post-Hoc Explainability

### 2.1 Post-Hoc Explainability
Applies an external algorithm to a trained black box to retroactively guess *why* it made a prediction.
- **Methods:** LIME [2], SHAP [3], Integrated Gradients [4].
- **Mechanism:** Computes feature attribution scores (e.g., "The word 'suicide' contributed $+0.4$ to the prediction").
- **Flaws:** Post-hoc explanations are notoriously fragile. They often explain the *model's artifacts* rather than the *true data generation process*, and they can be adversarially manipulated to hide biases [5].

### 2.2 Intrinsic Explainability
The model architecture itself forces the reasoning to be transparent by design.
- **Methods:** Decision Trees, Linear Regression, $k$-Nearest Neighbors.
- **Mechanism:** The user can trace the exact mathematical path that led to the output.

## 3. Example-Based Explanations and Their Limitations

Because $k$-Nearest Neighbors is an intrinsically interpretable model, a reliability score grounded in retrieved neighbors comes with a built-in explanation: the neighbors themselves. While these intrinsic explanations bypass the fragility of SHAP, they introduce a distinct set of risks that must be addressed before deployment.

### 3.1 Neighbor Leakage
Nearest neighbors are literal extractions from the training data. If the system retrieves a historical clinical note, it risks leaking **Protected Health Information (PHI)** or other sensitive data. Strict anonymization of the non-parametric memory bank is absolutely required to ensure HIPAA compliance.

### 3.2 Retrieval Artifacts
Bad neighbors create bad explanations. If the embedding space is poorly optimized (e.g., severe Anisotropy or Hubness as discussed in Note 17), the system will retrieve neighbors that are mathematically close but semantically nonsensical.
```text
Bad Retrieval → Bad Explanation → Loss of Clinical Trust
```

### 3.3 Correlation ≠ Causation
Neighbor examples explain *why the model is uncertain*, not necessarily *why the true label exists*. For instance, if the model retrieves three neighbors labeled "Depression" that all mention "taking a walk," the user might falsely assume "taking a walk" causes depression, when it is merely a spurious correlation captured by the geometry.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A predictive reliability estimator is not a causal model of model failure.**
> High JS Divergence strongly *correlates* with epistemic failure, but it does not strictly *cause* the failure in a mechanistic sense. The actual cause of failure is the model's parametric weights being misaligned with the true data distribution. A geometric reliability score simply uses the non-parametric geometry as a proxy to detect that misalignment. Clinicians must be trained to interpret such scores as an indicator of risk, not a diagnostic explanation of the pathology itself.

## 4. Comparing XAI for Reliability

| Method | Output | Clinical Actionability | Trustworthiness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Attention Weights [6]** | Heatmap over input tokens. | Low. (Attention is often not explanation). | Low. Can be manipulated. |
| **SHAP / LIME** | Token-level feature importance scores. | Moderate. Shows *where* the model looked, but not *why* it failed. | Moderate. Prone to instability. |
| **Concept Activation (TCAV) [8]** | "Prediction aligns with the concept of 'Lethargy'." | High. Highly interpretable semantic concepts. | High. |
| **Example-Based ($k$-NN Retrieval)** | The exact historical texts causing the geometric conflict. | **Highest.** The clinician can literally read the confusing priors. | **Potentially High.** Grounded strictly in retrieved evidence, not model approximations. |
