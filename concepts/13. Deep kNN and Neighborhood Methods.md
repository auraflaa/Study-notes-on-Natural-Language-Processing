## 1. Nearest Neighbor Methods in Modern AI

The $k$-Nearest Neighbor algorithm [1] is one of the oldest and simplest non-parametric classification methods in machine learning. Unlike parametric models (which compress all training data into a set of weight matrices $W$), $k$-NN memorizes the entire training dataset. At inference, it predicts the label of a test point based on a majority vote of its $k$ closest neighbors in a feature space.

**The Semantic Void of Raw $k$-NN:**
Historically, $k$-NN operated directly on raw features (e.g., pixel intensities or TF-IDF word counts). Because raw ambient spaces suffer massively from the curse of dimensionality and lack semantic clustering, classical $k$-NN failed on complex tasks.

**The Deep Renaissance:**
Deep neural networks act as exceptionally powerful feature extractors. When $k$-NN is applied not to raw inputs, but to the latent representations $z = f(x)$ of a trained deep network, the algorithm becomes highly competitive. The network unrolls the manifold (as discussed in Note 11), providing a smooth semantic space where the $k$-NN distance metric actually functions properly [2][3].

## 2. Deep k-Nearest Neighbors (DkNN)

The foundational work linking modern deep representations to $k$-NN for reliability estimation is **Deep k-Nearest Neighbors (DkNN)** by Papernot and McDaniel (2018) [4].

**The Mechanism:**
Instead of solely relying on the final softmax output, DkNN analyzes the neighborhood of the input at *every hidden layer* of the network. 
1. The training set representations are cached for every layer.
2. For a test input, its $k$-nearest neighbors are retrieved at layer 1, layer 2, ..., up to the final layer.
3. The empirical label distribution of the neighbors is computed at each layer.

**The Insight:**
Papernot et al. demonstrated that adversarial examples and Out-of-Distribution (OOD) samples suffer from **neighborhood breakdown**. A normal sample will retrieve consistent neighbors belonging to its true class across all layers. An adversarial sample might retrieve dog images at early layers, but is forced by the adversarial perturbation to retrieve cat images at the final layer.

DkNN uses the lack of conformity among retrieved neighbors to flag an input as unreliable.

## 3. Local Outlier Factor (LOF)

Before modern deep learning, the **Local Outlier Factor (LOF)** (Breunig et al., 2000) [5] established the mathematical foundation for using neighborhoods to detect anomalies.

Rather than looking at absolute distance (which fails if the overall density of the dataset varies), LOF computes the *local* density of a point compared to the local density of its $k$-neighbors. 
- If a point's density is much lower than its neighbors, it is an outlier (OOD).
- If a point's density is similar to its neighbors, it is an inlier (In-Distribution).

**Why this matters for neighborhood-based reliability signals:**
LOF introduced the concept of **relative density**. In high-dimensional transformer spaces, certain regions are inherently dense while others are sparse. Such methods cannot rely on an absolute distance threshold ($\text{cosine similarity} > 0.9$) to define a valid neighborhood; they must rely on the relative distribution ($Q_{kNN}$) and relative entropy, mimicking the philosophy of LOF applied to deep semantic spaces.

## 4. Retrieval-Augmented Classification and kNN-LM

A parallel evolution of deep neighborhood methods occurred in Natural Language Processing with models like **kNN-LM** (Khandelwal et al., 2019) [6] and Retrieval-Augmented Classification (RAC) [7].

**kNN-LM Mechanism:**
Standard Language Models predict the next token using a massive linear layer over the vocabulary. kNN-LM alters this by caching the latent representations of every token in the training corpus. At inference, it computes a standard softmax distribution $P_{model}$, but also retrieves the $k$-nearest neighbors from the training cache to form a second distribution $P_{kNN}$. The final prediction is an interpolation:
$$P_{final} = (1 - \lambda)P_{model} + \lambda P_{kNN}$$

**Generalization through Memorization:**
Khandelwal et al. proved that explicitly retrieving neighbors allows the model to correctly handle rare events, long-tail facts, and domain shifts without needing larger parametric weights. The explicit access to the neighborhood grounds the model's parametric guesses in empirical evidence.

## 5. k-NN for OOD and Calibration

Recent work explicitly ties $k$-NN to the problems of calibration and Overconfidence (discussed in Note 12). 

Models are notoriously overconfident on Out-of-Distribution text [8]. However, geometry is not easily fooled. If a network trained on financial news is given a medical document, the network's linear classifier might arbitrarily assign it to "Stock Crash" with $p=0.99$. But in the latent space, the medical document will land in a sparse void, far from any financial training clusters.

By evaluating the distance to the $k$-th nearest neighbor [9], or computing the neighborhood entropy, we can accurately detect that the sample is OOD and forcefully calibrate (lower) the model's confidence.

## 8. Weighted kNN

While standard $k$-NN uses simple majority voting, modern geometric methods rarely treat all neighbors equally. A neighbor located at cosine similarity 0.99 should contribute vastly more to the empirical distribution than a neighbor at 0.60.

**Mechanism:**
Instead of a uniform distribution $Q_{kNN} = \frac{1}{k} \sum_i y_i$, a distance-weighted formulation is generally preferable:
$$Q_{kNN} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k w_i y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^k w_i}$$
where weights are typically derived from exponentiated similarity or an RBF kernel: $w_i = \exp(-\text{dist}(z, z_i) / \tau)$.

**Implications of weighting:**
Weighting directly affects the computation of Density, Purity, Neighborhood Entropy, and JS Divergence. A heavily weighted $Q_{kNN}$ will have lower entropy if the closest neighbors share a class, even if the $k$-th neighbor is distant and anomalous. This provides a smoother and more robust signal than hard majority voting.

## 8. Choosing K

The note so far has treated $k$ as a fixed constant, but in practice, $k$ is a critical experimental variable that determines the resolution of the non-parametric memory.

- **Small $k$ (e.g., $k=5$):** Highly sensitive to local noise. The resulting $Q_{kNN}$ distribution will be sharp and sparse, often leading to artificially high JS Divergence.
- **Large $k$ (e.g., $k=100$):** Oversmoothed. The neighborhood expands to encompass multiple semantic clusters, turning $Q_{kNN}$ into the dataset's global prior rather than a local semantic signal.

**The Bias-Variance Tradeoff:**
Practitioners must identify the optimal $k$ that balances local specificity with statistical stability — a core component of any experimental design that uses $k$-NN.

**Hyperparameter Brittleness:**
A rigorous evaluation must answer: *Does the JSD metric collapse if $k$ changes slightly?* If $JSD$ computed at $k=10$ shows zero correlation with $JSD$ computed at $k=50$, the estimator is brittle. The stability of geometric divergence across different neighborhood sizes is a core threat to the validity of the method.

## 9. Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search

In theoretical discussions, we assume exact $k$-NN retrieval. However, computing exact pairwise distances against a training corpus of 100,000 to 1,000,000 vectors is computationally prohibitive for a real-time reliability API.

**Modern Retrieval:**
Production-scale systems typically rely on **Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN)** algorithms rather than exact search. Libraries like FAISS, using index types such as:
- **HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World):** Graph-based fast search.
- **IVF (Inverted File Index):** Voronoi-cell clustering.
- **PQ (Product Quantization):** Memory-efficient compressed vectors.

The use of ANN introduces a small amount of retrieval stochasticity; downstream features (like Entropy and JSD) must be robust enough to survive the approximations inherent in HNSW or IVF indices.

### 9.1 Computational Complexity & Latency Trade-offs
A critical reviewer question for any neighborhood-based estimator is the operational latency. While a standard Softmax evaluation is $\mathcal{O}(1)$ during inference, exact $k$-NN requires $\mathcal{O}(N \log k)$ operations per query, where $N$ is the dataset size.
- **The Bottleneck:** Computing $JSD(P_{model} || Q_{kNN})$ requires first computing the $k$ nearest neighbors in $d$-dimensional space (costing $\mathcal{O}(N \cdot d)$ without indexing).
- **The Trade-off:** The marginal gain in AUROC provided by geometric disagreement must justify this massive latency hit. For real-time applications (e.g., streaming API traffic), the latency introduced by a FAISS lookup might be unacceptable. Careful index quantization (e.g., IVFPQ) is mandatory to compress the representation cache and minimize I/O overhead.

## 10. Neighborhood Instability

Retrieval quality dictates the success of any neighborhood-based reliability estimator. But even with perfect exact search, representations are surprisingly fragile.

If we extract representations from two identical networks trained with different random seeds, the macro-level clusters remain stable (e.g., "Medical" texts stay far from "Sports" texts). However, the specific micro-level ordering of the top-$k$ nearest neighbors can shift drastically.

**Implications:**
Because nearest neighbors are not deterministic mathematical constants but artifacts of specific embedding trajectories, the $Q_{kNN}$ distribution is inherently noisy. This instability strictly necessitates the use of moderate $k$ (to smooth out individual neighbor swaps) and distance weighting (to down-weight unstable, distant neighbors).

## 11. Conformal Prediction Connection

A rapidly growing field in uncertainty quantification is **Conformal Prediction**, which provides mathematically guaranteed error bounds for predictions.

The core mechanism of Conformal Prediction relies on a **Nonconformity Score**, which measures how "unusual" a new test sample is compared to the calibration data. In Papernot's original DkNN paper [4], neighborhood evidence is explicitly formulated as a nonconformity score—if a sample's retrieved neighbors do not match the predicted class, the sample is highly nonconforming.

**Connection to Conformal Prediction:**
The JS Divergence between $P_{model}$ and $Q_{kNN}$ is a natural, highly robust nonconformity score that can be used within the Conformal Prediction framework to mathematically guarantee abstention rates.

## 12. The Trust Score Connection

As discussed extensively in Note 12, the **Trust Score** [9] is a foundational geometric reliability method that measures the distance from a test point to its predicted class centroid, divided by the distance to the nearest alternative centroid.

It is instructive to compare disagreement-based methods directly to Trust Scores. 

**Positioning the Literature:**
While both methods rely on deep representations, they extract fundamentally different signals:
- **Trust Score = Distance Confidence.** It asks: "How physically far am I from the prototype of my predicted class?"
- **Distributional Disagreement** asks: "How much does the local empirical distribution ($Q_{kNN}$) contradict the model's parametric output ($P_{model}$)?"

Trust Scores rely heavily on isotropic, well-separated Euclidean clusters. Measuring JS Divergence over the local empirical distribution, by contrast, captures uncertainty stemming from high-entropy, overlapping boundaries that raw centroid distances ignore.
