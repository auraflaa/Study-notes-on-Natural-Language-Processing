## 1. The Core Philosophy of Abstention

In standard machine learning, a model is forced to make a prediction for every single input $x$, regardless of its internal uncertainty. In high-stakes domains (like clinical mental health diagnosis), forcing a guess on an ambiguous input leads to catastrophic, high-cost errors.

Selective prediction allows the model to output "I don't know" (abstain) when it lacks sufficient epistemic confidence [1].

Let $f(x)$ be the standard classifier predicting $y \in \mathcal{Y}$.
Let $g(x) \in \{0, 1\}$ be the **Selection Function**.

The selective model $F(x)$ operates as:
$$F(x) = \begin{cases} 
f(x) & \text{if } g(x) = 1 \text{ (Predict)} \\
\text{Abstain} & \text{if } g(x) = 0 \text{ (Reject)} 
\end{cases}$$

**The Role of Reliability Signals:** The quality of abstention depends on the signals used to parameterize $g(x)$. Instead of relying purely on $p_{max}$, $g(x)$ can evaluate geometric disagreement ($JSD$).

### 1.1 Chow's Reject Rule (1970)
The foundation of modern abstention was established by C.K. Chow in 1970. Chow mathematically proved that the optimal rejection rule (minimizing risk for a given reject rate) is simply thresholding the posterior probability of the true class. 

If the cost of making an error is $C_e$ and the cost of rejecting is $C_r$, Chow defined the optimal decision rule $d(x)$ as:
$$d(x) = \begin{cases} 
\arg\max_y P(y|x) & \text{if } \max_y P(y|x) \ge 1 - \frac{C_r}{C_e} \\
\text{Reject} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}$$

Every modern method (SelectiveNet, Deep Gamblers, geometric disagreement scoring) is ultimately an attempt to find a better, more calibrated estimate of that posterior probability $P(y|x)$ when the model's raw Softmax fails to match Chow's ideal.

## 2. The Risk-Coverage Trade-off

Selective prediction is entirely governed by the mathematical tension between Risk and Coverage [2].

- **Coverage ($c$):** The fraction of the total dataset where $g(x) = 1$. (Higher is better for automation).
- **Selective Risk ($r_c$):** The error rate computed *only* on the covered subset. (Lower is better for safety).

### 2.1 The Mathematical Definition
Given a loss function $\ell(\hat{y}, y)$, the empirical Selective Risk at coverage $c$ is:
$$\hat{r}_c = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(f(x_i), y_i) \cdot g(x_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^n g(x_i)}$$

### 2.2 Comparison: Evaluating Selective Predictors

If we compare two abstention mechanisms (e.g., Softmax Confidence vs. Geometric Disagreement), how do we know which is better?

| Metric | Mechanism | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Risk-Coverage (RC) Curve** | Plot Selective Risk (y-axis) against Coverage (x-axis) across all possible thresholds. | A strictly lower RC curve means the scoring function filters errors more efficiently than Softmax. |
| **Area Under the RC Curve (AURC)** | The integral of the RC curve. | Lower AURC = Better selective predictor. |
| **Expected AURC (E-AURC)** | AURC normalized by the optimal possible AURC for that dataset. | The most rigorous baseline-independent metric for comparing reliability estimators. |
| **Risk at Fixed Coverage** | Fix coverage at $80\%$. What is the error rate? | "If we automate 80% of clinical notes, what is our false-positive rate?" |
| **Coverage at Fixed Risk** | Fix max allowable risk at $1\%$. How much of the dataset can we process? | "If we demand 99% accuracy, how many notes must be routed to humans?" |

## 3. Mechanisms for the Selection Function $g(x)$

How do we define $g(x)$? It almost universally requires a reliability scoring function $s(x)$ and a tunable threshold $\theta$.
$$g(x) = \mathbb{I}(s(x) \geq \theta)$$

### 3.1 Comparison: Selection Functions

| Method | Scoring Function $s(x)$ | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Max Softmax (Baseline)** | $p_{max}$ | Subject to severe overconfidence and calibration drift. |
| **Monte Carlo Dropout** | Variance of predictions across $N$ stochastic forward passes. | Computationally prohibitive for real-time LLM inference. |
| **Deep Ensembles** | Agreement across $N$ independently trained models. | $O(N)$ training and inference cost. |
| **Deep Gamblers** | Model learns a dedicated "abstention class" during training [4]. | Requires retraining the base model from scratch. |

Post-hoc geometric scoring (e.g., a $k$-NN lookup over the training set with JS Divergence against $Q_{kNN}$) is attractive because it requires only one forward pass and zero retraining of the base model.

## 4. The Cost-Sensitive Abstention Framework

In clinical settings, errors and human labor have different financial and ethical costs [6].
Let $C_e$ be the cost of an error (e.g., misdiagnosing a crisis).
Let $C_a$ be the cost of abstention (e.g., paying a human clinician to review the note).

A prediction should only be made if the expected cost of predicting is strictly less than the cost of abstaining:
$$P(\text{Error} | x) \cdot C_e < C_a$$

Rewriting this, the optimal abstention threshold is defined by the cost ratio:
$$P(\text{Error} | x) < \frac{C_a}{C_e}$$

**Connecting Signal Detection to Cost-Sensitive Abstention:**
A logistic regression calibrated on geometric features ($JSD$) can produce an accurate estimate of $P(\text{Error} | x)$, which can then be plugged directly into the cost-sensitive equation above to optimally minimize total clinical cost.

## 5. Calibration and Abstention

Abstention quality is inextricably linked to model calibration (Note 7). The mathematical relationship is direct:
```text
Poor Calibration → Misaligned P(y|x) → Suboptimal Chow Thresholds → Degraded Abstention
```
If a model's confidence scores do not reflect true probabilities (e.g., it assigns $p_{max} = 0.99$ to instances where it is only $60\%$ accurate), any global threshold $\theta$ will reject the wrong samples. It will reject accurate predictions that happen to have low confidence, while confidently accepting errors. 

**The Geometric Advantage:**
Replacing the poorly calibrated Softmax $p_{max}$ with the geometrically grounded $JSD$ yields a scoring function that naturally correlates with the true likelihood of error. While JSD does not eliminate the need for calibration entirely, it **may significantly reduce dependence on parametric calibration** (like Temperature Scaling), which often fails under distribution shift.

## 6. Pathologies in Selective Prediction

### 6.1 The Slice Pathology
When $g(x)$ filters out hard examples, it often disproportionately filters out specific demographic groups or rare linguistic variations (semantic outliers). This can artificially boost the overall metric while creating severe fairness/bias issues. Any system that abstains based on geometric isolation must evaluate whether it disproportionately affects minority dialects.

### 6.2 The Threshold Shift
A threshold $\theta$ calibrated on an evaluation set may fail in production if the distribution shifts (Covariate Shift). Non-parametric $k$-NN naturally resists this: if the test distribution shifts away from the training distribution, the test points will land in sparse geometric space, naturally lowering their reliability scores without requiring a threshold update.
