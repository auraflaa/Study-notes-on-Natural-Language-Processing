## 1. The Overconfidence Problem

Modern deep neural networks are highly accurate, but they are pathologically overconfident [1]. A classical neural network (e.g., LeNet from 1998) produced prediction probabilities that roughly matched true accuracy—if the network predicted $p=0.80$, it was right about 80% of the time. 

Modern architectures (e.g., ResNets, Transformers) routinely predict $p=0.99$ even when their true accuracy on those samples is $60\%$. This is primarily caused by:
1. **Model Capacity:** Massive parameter counts allow networks to memorize datasets and easily separate classes with wide margins.
2. **Proper Scoring Rules:** As discussed in Note 10, the extreme asymmetric penalty of Cross-Entropy Loss forces models to aggressively push logits apart, artificially inflating the softmax output.

For high-stakes domains (e.g., mental health triaging), knowing *when* a model is likely to fail is just as important as the overall accuracy [2].

## 2. Accuracy vs Calibration vs Reliability

It is critical to formally distinguish between these three concepts in the literature.

| Concept | Question Answered | Granularity | Metric |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Accuracy** | "How often is the model right?" | Dataset-level | Accuracy, F1, AUROC |
| **Calibration** | "Does $p=0.8$ mean 80% chance of being right?" | Bin-level (Macro) | Expected Calibration Error (ECE) [1] |
| **Reliability** | "Should I trust *this specific* prediction?" | Sample-level (Micro) | Rejection Rate, Risk-Coverage Curve |

**The Failure of Calibration:**
Calibration techniques like Temperature Scaling mathematically divide all logits by a learned scalar $T$. This perfectly flattens the distribution and improves the dataset-level ECE. However, it preserves the exact rank order of the original logits. **If the model was originally overconfident about the wrong class, Temperature Scaling will still predict the wrong class**, just with slightly less confidence. 

**Reliability estimation** seeks to identify *individual* sample-level failures that post-hoc calibration misses by evaluating entirely independent evidence (like geometry) [3].

## 3. Deep Ensembles: The Gold Standard

The most robust, empirically proven method for estimating predictive uncertainty and reliability under distribution shift is **Deep Ensembles** [4][5].

Introduced by Lakshminarayanan et al. (2017), a deep ensemble trains $M$ identical neural networks with different random weight initializations and random data shuffling. At inference, the output is the averaged probability distribution:
$$P_{ensemble}(y|x) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M P_{\theta_m}(y|x)$$

**Why it works:** Different initializations converge to different local optima. On clean, in-distribution data, all models agree (low variance, low entropy). On ambiguous or out-of-distribution data, the models disagree wildly (high variance, high entropy).

**The Problem:** Ensembles require $M$ times the computational cost for both training and inference. Deploying an ensemble of 5 BERTs for a real-time clinical API is often financially and practically prohibitive. 

*Lightweight geometric $k$-NN lookups are attractive because they can approximate some of the epistemic uncertainty detection of a Deep Ensemble using only a single forward pass.*

## 4. Learning Confidence (ConfidNet)

Rather than relying on the Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP), some approaches attempt to explicitly train a network to output its own confidence. 

**ConfidNet** [6] (Corbiere et al., 2019) adds a parallel branch to the neural network architecture that shares the representation layers but outputs a single scalar $c \in [0, 1]$ representing the True Class Probability (TCP). The network is trained using an MSE loss against the true correctness of the main classification branch.

**Limitations:**
While elegant, ConfidNet still relies on the network's own internal representations to judge itself. It suffers from the same OOD blindness as the primary classifier—if the network hasn't seen a specific type of noise, the confidence branch won't know how to evaluate it.

## 5. Geometric Confidence: The Trust Score

The foundational geometric method in this literature is the **Trust Score**, introduced by Jiang et al. (2018) [7]. 

The Trust Score abandons the output logits entirely and evaluates reliability entirely in the representation space (the unrolled manifold). 

**The Algorithm:**
1. Compute the geometric centroid (or filtered $k$-NN cluster) for every class in the training set.
2. For a test sample $x$, extract its representation $z$.
3. Find the predicted class $\hat{y}$.
4. Compute the distance from $z$ to the *predicted* class cluster: $D_{pred}$.
5. Compute the distance from $z$ to the *nearest alternative* class cluster: $D_{alt}$.
6. The Trust Score is the ratio:
$$\text{Trust Score} = \frac{D_{alt}}{D_{pred}}$$

If the Trust Score is high, the sample is securely embedded inside the predicted class cluster. If it is low ($< 1$), the sample is actually closer to a completely different class, indicating a severe failure of the linear classification layer.

**Limitations of Trust Scores:**
1. **Purely Distance-Based:** It relies strictly on Euclidean or Cosine distance to class centers, ignoring the density, entropy, and complex manifold shapes of the local neighborhood.
2. **Ignores Model Disagreement:** It provides a separate geometric score, but it does not formally measure the *information-theoretic divergence* between the model's stated confidence and the geometric reality.
