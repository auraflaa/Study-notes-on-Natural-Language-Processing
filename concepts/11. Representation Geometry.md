# Representation Geometry

These are comprehensive study notes. Every factual claim is cited. References focus on deep representation learning, the manifold hypothesis, and the mathematical properties of contrastive representation spaces.

The fundamental question any neighborhood-based reliability method must answer is: **Why should neighbors mean anything at all?** When we retrieve the $k$-nearest neighbors of a sample in the latent space of a neural network, why do we assume their labels provide a reliable signal about the ground truth?

This note explores the mathematical and geometric properties of deep neural network representations that guarantee semantic structure.

## 1. The Manifold Hypothesis

The fundamental assumption underlying all deep representation learning—and thus all neighborhood-based reliability estimation—is the **Manifold Hypothesis** [1][6].

High-dimensional real-world data (such as raw text or pixel arrays) does not uniformly populate its high-dimensional ambient space. Instead, the data is concentrated on or near a lower-dimensional continuous manifold (or a set of manifolds) embedded within that high-dimensional space.

A deep neural network (such as BERT) acts as a sequence of differentiable, non-linear transformations that effectively "unrolls" or "flattens" these tangled manifolds. The final representation space $Z \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (where $d=768$ for BERT) is a space where the underlying semantic factors of variation have been mapped into linear (or near-linear) geometric relationships.

Because data points sharing similar underlying semantics lie close together on the manifold, they are mapped to geometrically adjacent coordinates in $Z$. This is why searching for $k$-nearest neighbors retrieves semantically related samples.

## 2. Local vs. Global Structure

In representation learning, there is a strict mathematical distinction between local and global structure [7][8].

- **Global Structure:** The absolute coordinates of a point $z$ or the long-range distances across the entire space. Global structure is highly dependent on initialization, optimization trajectories, and random seed. Two different runs of the same model may result in completely different global coordinate systems (e.g., rotated or translated).
- **Local Structure:** The immediate geometric neighborhood around a point $z$. Which points are closest to $z$?

**Invariance of Local Structure:** 
While global structure is unstable, local structure is highly conserved across different neural networks trained on the same task [9][10]. Techniques like Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) prove that networks reliably learn the same local similarity structures regardless of architecture [11].

For reliability estimation, this means that while we cannot assign meaning to the absolute vector $[0.1, -0.4, \dots]$, we can absolutely assign meaning to the set of $k$-nearest neighbors surrounding that vector. The reliability signal resides in the *local structure*.

## 3. Semantic Similarity and Distance Metrics

In high-dimensional spaces ($d \ge 768$), distance metrics behave counter-intuitively due to the "curse of dimensionality." 

### 3.1 Comparison: Distance Metrics in Representation Space
When evaluating local neighborhoods, the choice of metric radically alters the retrieved semantics.

| Metric | Mechanism | Robust to Scaling? | Accounts for Covariance? | Typical Use Case |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Euclidean ($\ell_2$)** | Direct point-to-point spatial distance. | No. Magnitude heavily influences distance. | No. Assumes spherical distribution. | Low-dimensional physics, basic ML. |
| **Cosine Similarity** | Angular distance (dot product of $\ell_2$-normalized vectors). | Yes. Focuses purely on directionality. | No. | Contrastive learning, Transformer Attention, deep k-NN classifiers. |
| **Mahalanobis Distance** | Measures distance relative to the distribution's covariance matrix. | Yes. | Yes. Stretches axes where variance is high. | Advanced OOD detection (e.g., Lee et al., 2018). |

**Why Cosine Similarity is preferred:**
In contrastive learning frameworks and transformer attention layers, the dot product directly dictates the flow of information. Therefore, semantic similarity is mathematically encoded angularly rather than volumetrically. Euclidean distance is fundamentally mismatched to how transformers construct their latent spaces.

## 4. Contrastive Learning Intuition

The explicit construction of semantically clustered representation spaces is formalized in **Contrastive Learning**, best exemplified by frameworks like SimCLR [2] and SimCSE [4].

The intuition is straightforward:
1. Construct a "positive pair" $(x_i, x_i^+)$ which share the same semantics (e.g., two augmented views of the same image, or a text sequence paired with itself via dropout).
2. Construct "negative pairs" $(x_i, x_j^-)$ which are semantically distinct.
3. Optimize the network to maximize the similarity of positive pairs while minimizing the similarity to negative pairs.

The standard contrastive objective (InfoNCE loss) for a batch of $N$ pairs is:
$$\mathcal{L}_{i,j} = -\log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(z_i, z_j)/\tau)}{\sum_{k=1}^{2N} \mathbb{1}_{[k \neq i]} \exp(\text{sim}(z_i, z_k)/\tau)}$$
where $\tau$ is a temperature hyperparameter.

### 4.1 Comparison: Contrastive Learning vs Masked Language Modeling (MLM)
An out-of-the-box BERT model trained on MLM (predicting missing words) produces incredibly poor sentence representations. Why?

| Objective | Mechanism | Latent Space Geometry | Suitability for k-NN Reliability Methods |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **MLM (Standard BERT)** | Predict missing tokens using contextual history. | Highly anisotropic (narrow cone). Local neighbors share syntax, not semantics. | **Poor.** Semantic neighborhoods are noisy. |
| **Contrastive (SimCSE)** | Pull semantically similar full sequences together. | Isotropic (spread out). Uniformity is explicitly enforced. | **Excellent.** Neighborhoods reliably reflect semantic classes. |

This is why neighborhood-based reliability methods must operate on representations explicitly fine-tuned for semantic clustering, rather than raw pre-trained token encodings.

## 5. Alignment and Uniformity

Wang and Isola (2020) [3] provided the canonical mathematical framework for *why* contrastive learning produces such excellent representations. They proved that the InfoNCE loss optimizes two distinct geometric properties asymptotically:

### 5.1 Alignment
Positive pairs should be mapped to the exact same point in the latent space.
$$\mathcal{L}_{align} \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{(x, x^+) \sim P_{pos}} \left[ \| f(x) - f(x^+) \|_2^\alpha \right]$$
High alignment means the network is invariant to noise; semantic identity maps to a tightly bound coordinate.

### 5.2 Uniformity
To prevent the trivial solution (mapping all inputs to a single point), the network must push distinct features apart. A good representation space should uniformly distribute embeddings across the hypersphere $S^{d-1}$, maximizing the preservation of information.
$$\mathcal{L}_{uniform} \triangleq \log \mathbb{E}_{x, y \sim P_{data}} \left[ \exp(-t \| f(x) - f(y) \|_2^2) \right]$$

**The Mechanism of Neighborhoods:**
Uniformity ensures that the high-dimensional space is maximally utilized, while Alignment ensures that semantically identical points cluster tightly. 
**Contrastive objectives encourage semantically related samples to occupy nearby regions, making local neighborhoods more likely to reflect semantic similarity.**

## 6. Intrinsic Dimensionality and Hubness

As dimensionality increases ($d=768, 1024, 3072$), distance metrics experience the "curse of dimensionality." Pairwise distances between points concentrate, and nearest-neighbor behavior becomes strange [12].

### 6.1 Intrinsic vs Ambient Dimension
While a BERT vector has $d=768$ (Ambient Dimension), the data manifold itself may only possess 10-20 degrees of semantic freedom (Intrinsic Dimension). The mismatch between ambient and intrinsic dimension is the root cause of many geometric artifacts.

### 6.2 Local Intrinsic Dimensionality (LID)
While global intrinsic dimension measures the manifold as a whole, **Local Intrinsic Dimensionality (LID)** measures the expansion rate of the data distribution within the immediate local neighborhood of a specific test point. 
LID has been proven highly effective in OOD detection and adversarial robustness. If a sample is embedded in a region with abnormally high LID, it implies the local manifold is "unfolding" or scattering in too many directions, indicating high epistemic uncertainty. LID serves as an advanced geometric feature that directly captures the "shape" of the local uncertainty.

### 6.3 Hubness
A major pathology in high-dimensional spaces is **Hubness**. Certain points—known as "hubs"—emerge as universal nearest neighbors to a vast number of other points in the dataset. 

**Implications for disagreement features:**
Hubs severely distort neighborhood-based signals. If a test point retrieves hubs instead of true semantic neighbors, it artificially skews the retrieved $Q_{kNN}$. This directly distorts Density, Purity, Neighborhood Entropy, and JS Divergence. Recognizing and mitigating hubness (e.g., via local scaling or mutual nearest neighbors) is critical for extracting clean disagreement features.

## 7. Embedding Anisotropy

Modern transformer embeddings are not uniformly distributed across the hypersphere. A famous result by Ethayarajh (2019) [13] proved that contextual embeddings are highly **anisotropic**—they occupy a surprisingly narrow cone in the representation space.

Because all representations are clustered in this narrow cone, the average cosine similarity between even completely unrelated random words is surprisingly high (often $>0.9$). 

**Implications for neighborhood quality:**
Anisotropy compresses the dynamic range of cosine similarities. It affects neighborhood quality and density estimates, requiring practitioners to rely on relative ranking ($k$-NN) or calibrated temperature scaling rather than absolute cosine similarity thresholds to define neighborhood boundaries.

## 8. Neighborhood Instability

A critical question is: *How stable are neighbors?* If we extract representations from BERT, RoBERTa, and DeBERTa, or if we simply retrain the same model with a different random seed, do the $k$-nearest neighbors remain the same?

Research shows that **neighborhood overlap is surprisingly fragile**. While macro-level clusters remain stable (e.g., "Medical" texts stay far from "Sports" texts), the specific micro-level ordering of the top-$k$ nearest neighbors can shift drastically depending on the model architecture and training seed.

**Implications:**
Because nearest neighbors are not deterministic mathematical constants but artifacts of specific embedding spaces, the $Q_{kNN}$ distribution is inherently noisy. To ensure robustness, practitioners should ideally use:
1. Well-calibrated retrieval embeddings (like SimCSE) rather than raw MLM encoders.
2. Moderate values of $k$ (e.g., $k=20$ or $k=50$) to smooth out the noise of individual neighbor swaps.
3. Density-weighted voting (scaling votes by cosine similarity) to reduce the impact of unstable, distant neighbors.

## 9. Trust Scores and Deep kNN

The use of geometric neighborhoods for reliability estimation builds upon a strong foundation of prior literature linking distance to confidence.

### 9.1 Comparison: Paradigms of Reliability Estimation
How does the geometric approach differ from traditional calibration?

| Paradigm | Mechanism | Example Method | Vulnerability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Logit-Based** | Rescales the raw Softmax output. | Temperature Scaling | Fails silently on Out-of-Distribution data. Cannot fix underlying rank errors. |
| **Distance-Based** | Measures geometric distance to the predicted class cluster vs other clusters. | Trust Score [14] | Ignores the mathematical disagreement with the model's actual stated confidence. |
| **Disagreement-Based** | Measures divergence between the model's output distribution ($P$) and the geometric neighborhood distribution ($Q$). | JS Divergence over neighborhood distributions | Heavily dependent on retrieval quality (embedding capacity). |

**The Disagreement-Based Distinction:**
Disagreement-based methods ask whether *information-theoretic disagreement* adds novel predictive information beyond simple distance-based confidence. They ask whether the model's explicitly stated belief contradicts the empirical neighborhood distribution.

## 10. Neighborhood Quality and Retrieval Evaluation

The note implicitly assumes that a "good embedding" yields a "good neighborhood." However, this is not guaranteed.

Neighborhood preservation depends strictly on retrieval quality. If the underlying encoder (e.g., BERT) lacks the capacity or tuning to partition a specific semantic space (like clinical mental health language), the retrieved neighbors will be noisy, and any downstream reliability signal will collapse.

Embeddings must be rigorously evaluated on standardized retrieval benchmarks like **MTEB** (Massive Text Embedding Benchmark) [16] and **BEIR** [17]. High performance on these benchmarks ensures the representations possess the necessary retrieval quality to support downstream reliability features.
